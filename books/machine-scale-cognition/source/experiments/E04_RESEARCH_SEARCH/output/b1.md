Conductivity loss in a solid-state battery is usually not a single “electrolyte conductivity” problem. The measured cell resistance can rise because of bulk electrolyte degradation, growing interphases, loss of solid–solid contact, current constriction, cracking, or electrode reaction limitations. The first decision is therefore to localize the resistance increase.

## Leading mechanisms

| Mechanism | Physical origin | Expected signature |
|---|---|---|
| Interfacial chemical decomposition | Reduction of the solid electrolyte at Li, oxidation at high-voltage cathodes, and reaction with conductive carbon or active material. Sulfide electrolytes may form Li₂S, polysulfides, phosphates, LiCl, transition-metal sulfides, and electronically conductive products. | Persistent interfacial impedance growth; chemically altered layers at the electrode/electrolyte boundary; weak recovery with stack pressure. |
| Space-charge or Li-depleted interphase | Chemical-potential mismatch creates a Li-ion-depleted region and/or a decomposition-derived interphase at the cathode/SSE interface. | Strong dependence on cathode chemistry, coating, and voltage; impedance may appear early and increase with upper cutoff voltage. |
| Mechanical contact loss | Electrode expansion/contraction, particle fracture, delamination, creep, and void formation reduce the active solid–solid contact area. Current then concentrates through remaining contact points. | Resistance is pressure-sensitive and may partially recover under compression; capacity loss correlates with swelling, void volume, or particle separation. |
| Li-metal stripping/deposition damage | During stripping, Li can fail to flow fast enough to refill the interface, creating voids. During deposition, current constriction and cracks can promote filamentary Li penetration and short circuiting. | Anode interfacial resistance rises during stripping; abrupt changes can correlate with pressure, current density, and plated/stripped capacity. |
| Bulk electrolyte or grain-boundary degradation | Cracking, impurity formation, densification changes, moisture/chemical attack, or grain-boundary reactions increase the intrinsic ionic resistance. | High-frequency/bulk resistance increases even in symmetric or blocking-electrode cells; weak sensitivity to applied stack pressure compared with interfacial resistance. |
| Cathode-network degradation | Cathode cracking, loss of electronic percolation, active-material isolation, and heterogeneous lithiation create reaction-limited regions that appear as increased polarization or apparent impedance. | Rate-dependent capacity loss, spatially heterogeneous state of charge, and cathode-side impedance growth without necessarily large bulk-SSE changes. |

There is strong direct evidence for several of these pathways. Operando X-ray tomography of Li/Li₁₀SnP₂S₁₂ cells directly observed void formation during stripping and identified loss of Li/SSE contact and current constriction as the primary failure cause in that system. [Nature Materials: “Linking void and interphase evolution to electrochemistry in solid-state batteries”](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-020-00903-2)

At cathodes, cycling-induced volume changes and mechanical deflation have been linked to increasing interfacial ion-transport resistance and charge heterogeneity. [Nature Communications: “Insights into interfacial effect and local lithium-ion transport in polycrystalline cathodes of solid-state batteries”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19528-9) Grain-level work further attributes contact loss to crystallographic anisotropy, SOC-dependent diffusion, and cathode dislocation formation. [Nature Communications, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-52123-w)

Chemical interphase growth is also experimentally supported. In Si/Li₆PS₅Cl composite anodes, growth of the solid-electrolyte interphase at the Si/SSE interface was reported to cause severe resistance increase and rapid capacity decay. [Nature Materials: “Chemo-mechanical failure mechanisms of the silicon anode in solid-state batteries”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-023-01792-x) Sulfide-electrolyte reviews summarize XPS and related evidence for sulfur, polysulfide, LiCl, and phosphate decomposition products at oxide-cathode interfaces. [Sulfide solid electrolytes review](https://doi.org/10.1002/aenm.201800933)

## Competing explanations and how to distinguish them

A rising low-frequency arc alone does not prove chemical decomposition. It can also result from shrinking contact area or a changing reaction distribution. Conversely, a pressure-sensitive resistance does not exclude chemistry: pressure can mask a chemically resistive layer by increasing the number of surviving contact points.

Useful discriminants are:

- Bulk electrolyte failure: high-frequency resistance rises in a blocking-electrode or symmetric-electrolyte control.
- Interfacial decomposition: the resistance remains after controlled compression and correlates with a chemically distinct interphase.
- Contact loss/voids: resistance changes reversibly or hysteretically with pressure and tracks observed void/contact evolution.
- Cathode fracture or reaction heterogeneity: impedance and capacity become strongly SOC/rate dependent, with spatially nonuniform lithiation.
- Li-metal failure: degradation is asymmetric between Li and cathode half-cells and is particularly sensitive to stripping current density, areal capacity, and stack pressure.

## The three most discriminating experiments

### 1. Operando imaging plus synchronized electrochemical impedance

Use synchrotron X-ray microtomography, or—where resolution and geometry permit—operando optical imaging/dilatometry, while cycling a Li/SSE/Li symmetric cell and a representative full cell. Record impedance or current-interruption resistance at defined SOCs.

Measure:

- interfacial void volume and contact area;
- electrode thickness/stack displacement;
- crack formation and propagation;
- resistance versus time, SOC, stripped capacity, and pressure.

Interpretation:

- Resistance rises immediately after visible stripping voids: mechanical contact loss is leading.
- Resistance rises while contact remains intact, followed by a chemically altered interface: interphase growth is leading.
- Cracks or Li penetration precede the impedance jump or short: fracture/filament growth is leading.

This is the strongest experiment for separating contact loss from purely chemical resistance because it measures the geometry that controls current constriction directly. The Li/SSE tomography study above is the clearest precedent.

### 2. Pressure-perturbation EIS with three-electrode or half-cell localization

Cycle matched cells under controlled stack pressures—ideally with several pressure steps—and measure EIS during cycling using:

- Li|SSE|Li symmetric cells;
- cathode|SSE|Li or cathode|SSE|In half-cells;
- a three-electrode architecture if available.

Fit the spectra with physically constrained equivalent circuits or transmission-line models, separating bulk SSE, anode interface, cathode interface, and charge-transfer/transport components.

Interpretation:

- Large reversible resistance reduction on pressure increase: contact/constriction resistance.
- Persistent resistance after pressure restoration: irreversible interphase growth, fracture, or chemical damage.
- Bulk resistance changing independently of pressure: electrolyte or grain-boundary degradation.
- Anode-side change in Li symmetric cells but not cathode half-cells: Li stripping/deposition damage.
- Cathode-side change that scales with upper cutoff voltage: oxidative cathode/SSE chemistry or cathode-side space-charge/interphase effects.

Pressure is not merely a cell-assembly variable: controlled-pressure studies have shown that both anode and cathode interfacial impedances can increase substantially as pressure is reduced, while the bulk SSE changes less. [Advanced Energy Materials, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/aenm.202304327) Pressure dependence therefore provides a practical way to quantify the mechanically recoverable fraction of the resistance.

### 3. Spatially resolved post-mortem chemistry and structure

Stop matched cells at beginning of life, after early degradation, and after severe degradation. Analyze both electrode interfaces and the electrolyte bulk using a combination such as:

- cryogenic XPS or ToF-SIMS for interphase composition and depth;
- cross-sectional STEM-EDS/EELS or atom-probe methods for nanoscale reaction layers;
- X-ray absorption spectroscopy for sulfide/phosphate structural changes;
- FIB-SEM or electron tomography for cracks, pores, and delamination.

Use uncycled controls and chemically/ mechanically isolated controls: electrolyte held against Li, cathode, and carbon separately.

Interpretation:

- A thickening, compositionally distinct interphase localized at one interface supports chemical decomposition.
- Transition-metal/S/P interdiffusion supports cathode/SSE reaction rather than simple contact loss.
- Extensive cracks and voids with little new interphase support mechanical degradation.
- Bulk compositional change or new grain-boundary phases supports intrinsic electrolyte degradation.
- A thin interphase plus large loss of physical contact indicates a coupled mechanism; do not force a single-cause interpretation.

## Decision rule

If only three experiments are possible, use them in this order:

1. **Operando imaging + synchronized impedance** — establishes whether resistance follows voids, cracks, or contact evolution.
2. **Controlled-pressure, localized EIS** — separates reversible constriction/contact resistance from irreversible chemical and bulk contributions.
3. **Spatially resolved cryogenic spectroscopy/microscopy** — identifies the actual reaction products and distinguishes interphase growth from structural damage.

The most likely practical conclusion in many sulfide-based Li-metal cells is a coupled failure sequence: interfacial reaction and mechanical contact loss increase local current density; localized current then accelerates further decomposition, voiding, cracking, or Li penetration. The experiments should therefore quantify both chemistry and contact mechanics rather than treating “conductivity loss” as an intrinsic bulk-electrolyte property.
