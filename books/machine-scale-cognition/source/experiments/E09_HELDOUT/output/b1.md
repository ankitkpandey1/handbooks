With the conjecture, invariant, graph class, and observations unspecified, no mathematically meaningful verdict is possible. The most useful one-week decision procedure is:

- **Day 1:** Precisely state the conjecture, definitions, quantifiers, graph class, and computational-generation method. Check whether the 120 cases are independent and cover meaningful regimes.
- **Days 2–3:** Reproduce the computations and run systematic searches beyond the observed range, especially on boundary cases, exceptional families, disconnected graphs, small/large parameter values, and randomly generated instances.
- **Days 3–4:** Test structural consequences and known extremal families. Attempt reductions to established theorems; use symbolic computation or exhaustive enumeration where feasible.
- **Days 4–5:** Try proof strategies suggested by the invariant: induction, extremal counterexample, decomposition, duality, monotonicity, probabilistic arguments, or classification of equality cases.
- **Days 6–7:** Decide using explicit evidence:

  - **Pursue a proof** if the statement survives adversarial searches, has a coherent mechanism, and reduces to a tractable lemma or known framework.
  - **Seek counterexamples** if failures cluster near natural boundaries, the invariant lacks monotonicity, or computational scaling reveals suspicious exceptions.
  - **Reformulate** if the pattern is broadly right but requires omitted hypotheses, an additive/multiplicative correction, asymptotic language, or restriction to a better-behaved graph class.

The default decision should be **not “prove it” merely because 120 examples agree**. First convert the observations into a precise, falsifiable statement and spend roughly half the week trying to break it. A surviving conjecture with an identified proof mechanism merits pursuit; otherwise, preserve the strongest verified version as the reformulated conjecture.
